That openness is why whale tracking exists, and why it's easy to misread. This guide covers what the data shows, how to track large wallets, and where copying them goes wrong. Last checked: October 2026. The whale everyone knows The best-documented case is "Théo", a French trader who bet heavily on Donald Trump in the 2024 US election. Chainalysis linked 11 accounts to him, including Fredi9999, Theo4, PrincessCaro and Michie, and Bloomberg's analysis put his profit at about $85 million ( Bloomberg ). What made it work was not the size of the bet. He believed public polls were understating Trump's support, and he paid for his own polling using the "neighbor effect": asking people who their neighbors would vote for, rather than who they would ( Entrepreneur, reporting the WSJ interview ). That is the lesson worth keeping: big winners usually have an information edge you can't see from their wallet. You see the trade, not the reason for it. Most traders lose On-chain studies agree the profits are concentrated. An April 2026 analysis found about 84% of Polymarket traders were unprofitable , with a tiny share of addresses taking most of the gains ( Casino.org ). Figures vary with method. Counting only wallets with several resolved trades gives a higher share of winners. Either way, most wallets lose. So "follow the big money" only helps if you follow the right big money, and big is not the same as good. How to track whales 1. Use a whale alert tool. More options: all alert and tracking tools , and our copy trading bots roundup . What to look for in a wallet A long record. Many resolved positions, not one famous win. One big hit can be luck. Profit across different markets, not only one event or one category. Sizes that are sensible for the wallet. Steady position sizes say more about skill than one all-in bet. Real results, not open positions. Unrealized gains can vanish; judge resolved trades. Why copying underperforms Even a genuinely good wallet is harder to copy than it looks: You are late. By the time an alert reaches you, the price has often moved. You don't know the exit. Whales sell quietly; you find out after. You don't know the reason. If their edge was information, it may be gone by the time you act. Fees add up. Since 2026 most Polymarket markets charge a taker fee on orders that fill straight away; resting limit orders don't. See our fees guide . Treat whale activity as a research lead: a reason to look at a market, not a reason to buy it. Wallets to be wary of New wallets with sudden huge profits: one lucky bet, or information you won't have next time. One-market heroes: a big win in one event, losses everywhere else. "Profits" from buying near-certain outcomes: buying at 99¢ before a market settles looks like a high win rate and earns almost nothing. Claims you can't check on-chain. If a profit can't be traced to a wallet, don't count it. Bottom line Whale tracking is useful for spotting where informed money is moving. It doesn't replace your own reasoning about the market, and copying blindly mostly hands you the whale's risk without their edge. Browse all whale tracking and alert tools → Found this useful?
